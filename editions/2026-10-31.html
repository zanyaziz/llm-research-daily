Saturday edition · Training, injection defenses & agent guardrails

One loss for the whole pipeline — and one line to opt out of training

Friday’s papers ask whether the staged training pipeline is a mathematical necessity or just a habit, how to teach a model to tell instructions from data, and whether the guardrails we bolt onto agents actually guard anything. Plus: the labs agree on a single robots.txt line for opting out of AI training — mostly not live yet.

The lead paper. A CMU / Johns Hopkins team derives RL objectives as maximum-likelihood fits to success-conditioned distributions, so pretraining, midtraining, SFT and RL all run through one weighted likelihood loss on a time-varying data mixture — staged pipelines recovered as a special case. Unified configurations beat staged baselines at larger compute budgets on reasoning puzzles, though staged training still wins at some intermediate budgets, and the largest model tested is 0.76B parameters.

The security paper. LLMON separates instructions from data into distinct slots that must be invoked via explicit identifiers, trained on a 4.6B-token structured corpus. Indirect prompt-injection attack success falls from 53–100% to 0.02–5.6% on Granite-4.1 3B and 8B — with no architectural change. Two benchmark papers below make the complementary point: output-only checks would have waved through most plausible-but-wrong agent work, and a curated ontology used as a guardrail flags correct answers barely better than random.

Also today. DeepMind’s Institute argues solo-agent benchmarks measure the wrong object — economies run on organizations. And OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and (reported) Anthropic converge on one robots.txt token, __usage_ai-training, for opting out of AI training — with timelines stretching from “already implementing” to 2027.
